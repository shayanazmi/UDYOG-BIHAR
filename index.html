Reading the exodusIn plain words
What “palayan” means

Palayan is the word Bihar uses for leaving: not a holiday trip and not a career move, but the departure of a young man because the district he grew up in has no work he can live on. The paper calls it a curse because it repeats itself every generation. Families spend what they have on schooling, and the schooling then qualifies the child for a train ticket instead of a job at home.

How to read the map

Each coloured dot is a hub, a district or city where workers gather before they leave. Each arrow is one corridor towards a destination box. The percentages beside the boxes are shares of observed out-migration, so Delhi NCR and Punjab (38%) and Gujarat (32%) together take most of the flow. The arrows show direction and relative weight. They are not literal rail routes.

Why the money matters more than the people

About one rural household in four depends on remittances, the money sent home by a son or brother working in another state. For a typical household the arithmetic is thin: monthly earnings of roughly ₹14,900 against living costs of roughly ₹12,400 leave a cushion of only about ₹2,500. Take the migrant's money out of that picture and the cushion turns into a monthly deficit. The state is therefore not just losing workers. Its families have come to rely on the loss.

CORE DIAGNOSIS:

Measuring Bihar's economic vitality solely by headline unemployment (4.2%) is like using a thermometer that only checks whether a patient is breathing declaring someone with a 105° fever completely healthy.

In an agrarian state, illiterate workers cannot afford to be idle; they must work daily in subsistence agriculture just to eat. The 4.2% rate measures bare survival, not economic vitality.

WHY THE STATISTIC MISLEADS

Higher education creates a reservation wage: once a youth earns a diploma or degree, their family expects formal employment. They refuse manual agricultural labor and enter a queue for formal industrial or corporate jobs. Because Bihar's private industrial base is virtually missing, educated youth queue indefinitely for government exams or exit via Palayan—causing open youth unemployment to spike to 21.93% statewide.

Why one number liesIn plain words
How the official rate is built

The unemployment rate divides the number of people actively looking for work by the labour force, meaning everyone who is either working or looking. It does not divide by the whole population, so students, retirees and people caring for children at home do not count. The survey also counts anyone who did even an hour of paid work in the reference week as employed.

Why a poor state can report a low rate

A day labourer with no savings cannot afford to sit at home, so he takes whatever work appears, digging, carrying bricks or loading trucks. He shows up in the data as employed. A graduate whose family has spent years and borrowed money on her degree will not take a ₹200-a-day digging job, so she waits and looks. She shows up as unemployed. The low statewide rate is largely a measure of how little poor people can afford to wait. It is not a measure of how many good jobs exist.

Three causes found in the records

No paperwork. Almost two in three salaried workers have no written contract, which means a boss can dismiss them tomorrow with no notice, no severance and no pension.

Locked-out women. Fewer than one in eight urban women work or look for work. The main reason women give is unpaid household care, and the cities have almost no crèches or safe commuter transport.

A wage floor set by distant cities. The money a brother sends from Surat or Ludhiana sets the lowest local wage a graduate will accept. A job paying ₹6,000 or ₹7,000 with no contract is worse than staying home and studying for an exam. Economists call that lowest acceptable pay a “reservation wage.”

What this rules out

Because the cause is a shortage of formal jobs, more training courses alone cannot fix it. The young people are willing. The local economy is not offering them anything to say yes to.

What each question meansIn plain words

These seven lenses are borrowed from economics, engineering and decision science. Each one is a way of asking a question that stops us from accepting the first, easiest answer. Here is what each term means in everyday language, and what it did to the analysis.

First principles

Strip the problem down to what a person actually decides. A worker compares two things: what a local job pays against what he nets by leaving. Everything else is commentary. That comparison is what sets the minimum wage the plan must offer.

Inversion

Instead of asking how to make Bihar prosperous, ask what would guarantee failure. Years of red tape, cash handed over before any job exists, and subsidies that nobody checks are the usual answers. The plan is built by doing the opposite of each.

Theory of constraints

A chain is as strong as its weakest link, and a pipe delivers only what its narrowest point allows. Pumping more training, more loans or more schemes into a system does nothing until the pinch point is opened. Here the pinch is the long delay in getting land and power to a factory.

Second-order thinking

Ask what happens after the first effect. A written three-year contract does more than add a piece of paper. A worker who knows he will not be fired stops hoarding cash out of fear and starts spending on groceries, repairs and school fees. That spending creates further jobs and tax receipts.

Ergodicity and distributions

An average describes a crowd. It does not describe any one person. If your head is in a freezer and your feet are in an oven, your average temperature is comfortable. A 4.2% statewide rate sounds calm, but an individual graduate in the worst-hit district faces odds close to one in two.

The Pareto principle

Often about 80% of a problem sits in about 20% of the places. Educated unemployment is concentrated in a handful of districts, so a limited budget works best when it is concentrated there first and not spread thinly across all 37.

Cybernetic feedback loops

A thermostat works because it measures the room and acts on the reading automatically. The same idea applied to subsidies means money is released only when independent records, such as power meters and provident-fund deposits, prove that a firm is really operating and paying its workers. No file waits on one official's goodwill.

Reading the ladderIn plain words
The picture

Think of a ladder with three rungs. The bottom rung is the survival economy: farming, informal wage work and rural livelihoods. In it almost everybody is busy, which is why measured unemployment there is under one per cent. The middle rung is education and aspiration, where a young person acquires a degree, an ITI certificate or a polytechnic diploma. The top rung is formal employment: a contract, a stable income, social security and a career at home.

What is missing

In Bihar the rails exist but the link between the middle and top rungs has gone. That link is the industrial middle: factories and service firms that take an educated young person and give her a contract and a path. The result is the picture on either side of the ladder. Some young people wait in the queue, spending five to seven prime years in repeated exam cycles. Others take the train, and about 2.9 million active migrants are already doing so.

How the fix works

Step 04 rebuilds the missing rung rather than pushing people up past it. The state builds ready-to-use factory sheds so firms can start hiring within months. In return it asks for written three-year contracts at a wage that beats what a migrant nets in a distant city. When that rung is back, the climb from education to formal work can happen inside Bihar.

Reading the education chartIn plain words
Why we looked

Every parent in Bihar believes that studying hard leads to a secure life. We tested that promise by grouping workers by their highest level of schooling and calculating the unemployment rate for each group. The rate is the number of people seeking work divided by everyone who is working or seeking work in that group.

What the bars show

The rate does not fall as schooling rises. It climbs. For workers with no schooling it is about 0.5%, and for graduates it is 12.58%, roughly twenty-four times higher. For young people aged 15–29 with a degree or diploma the figure reaches 21.93%. In plain terms, of every hundred graduates who are ready and looking for work, about thirteen cannot find any.

The real-world story

Those with no schooling cannot afford to be unemployed, so they take any work, however poor. Graduates have a family that has invested in them, and they refuse jobs that waste the degree. The private economy offers very few formal jobs in between, so they wait, often at home, often for a government vacancy.

What follows

Sending more students to college without building employers for them only produces a larger educated queue. The plan therefore prioritises ready factory space in sectors that hire graduates, such as food processing, electronics assembly and back-office services, along with paid industrial internships.

Reading the age chartIn plain words
Why we looked

Official reports lump together teenagers, breadwinners in their forties and pensioners. That blends a very different set of experiences into one number. We isolated people aged 15 to 29 to see who is really carrying the burden.

What the bars show

Older workers look fine because most are settled in farming or small shops, mostly out of necessity. Among the young, joblessness is several times the state average, and the large majority of all unemployed people in Bihar are in this age band. The overall rate stays low because the young are a minority of the workforce and the older workers are mostly already absorbed into survival work.

The real-world story

The hardest step is the first formal job. Most graduates find no campus placements, no career fairs and no entry-level hiring pipeline. Many spend ages 20 to 28 in coaching rooms preparing for public-sector exams in which nearly everyone is turned down. If a young person has not found formal work by about 30, skills fade, confidence drops and informal labour becomes the only option.

What follows

A bridge is needed between campus and workplace. In the plan, firms in the industrial parks reserve a share of entry-level posts for paid apprenticeships, so that a fresh graduate has real experience and a placement route within months.

Reading the training chartIn plain words
Why we looked

Governments spend heavily on Industrial Training Institutes (ITIs) and vocational certificates, on the assumption that skilled people get hired. We compared unemployment among trained and untrained workers to check whether that assumption holds.

What the bars show

Workers with formal vocational training face an unemployment rate of 14.33%, compared with 3.89% among those with no training. A certified worker is therefore close to four times as likely to be out of work as an uncertified one.

The real-world story

Training centres have often been funded for how many certificates they issue, not how many students get hired. Students learn older trades on worn equipment and leave with expectations of a skilled wage. Local employers cannot use their skills, and the students are too qualified for casual labour and not qualified for modern work.

What follows

The fix is to make employers part of the classroom. Factory owners help design the syllabus, students spend about half their time on real production floors using modern machines, and public training money is released only when graduates are actually placed.

Reading the gender chartIn plain words
Why we looked

Bihar has spent years on girls’ education through stipends, free bicycles and fee waivers. We wanted to know whether those educated women are turning that education into paid work.

What the bars show

Not for the most part. Among workers with a college degree, the survey finds more than seventeen working men for every working woman, and more than nine in ten educated women are outside the formal workforce. The main reason, given by roughly two-thirds of women who do not work, is unpaid household work and caring for children or elders. Opposition from family or culture is the stated reason for fewer than one woman in twenty.

The real-world story

Women are not staying home for lack of ambition. The outside world is not built for them. Commuting alone on unsafe roads, factory gates with no lighting and workplaces with no childcare make staying home the rational choice for a family. When a woman does receive certified training, her pay rises sharply and gets close to that of trained men, which shows that the barrier is access and not ability.

What follows

Making women’s inclusion a requirement of every industrial park: free point-to-point shuttle buses, an on-site certified crèche, flexible four- and six-hour shifts and training seats reserved for women with hiring agreements attached.

Reading the contract chartIn plain words
Why we looked

Official statistics label anyone who receives a regular monthly payment a “salaried employee,” which sounds secure. We checked how many of them actually hold a signed contract and how long it runs.

What the bars show

About 62% of salaried workers have no written contract. Roughly 31% hold one that runs more than three years, and only a small remainder have short ones. Pay climbs steeply with the length of the agreement: from about ₹12,850 a month with no contract to over ₹35,000 with a contract longer than three years, close to three times as much.

The real-world story

A firm that hires someone for three months treats her as disposable and has no reason to train her. A firm that signs her for three years invests in her skills, promotes her into supervision and pays for the productivity that results. Stability produces productivity and productivity produces higher wages. A contract also lets a worker prove her income to a bank. Without it she borrows from moneylenders who charge very high rates.

What follows

The state ties its support to contract length. Firms that offer three-year contracts get a share of their provident-fund and insurance costs reimbursed, so long-term hiring becomes the cheaper choice.

Reading the safety-net chartIn plain words
Terms used here

ESI is the Employees’ State Insurance scheme, which covers health care and sickness benefit. PF is the provident fund, a retirement savings account to which employer and employee both contribute. EPFO is the body that administers PF. A salaried job that includes both is the closest thing to a safety net most workers can have.

What the bars show

Roughly four in five salaried workers have no health cover, about three in four have no provident fund or pension, and more than two in three have no paid sick leave. Fewer than one in four has any financial cushion for illness or old age.

The real-world story

When an uncontracted worker is injured, nobody pays him. His family borrows from a moneylender at ruinous interest or sells land to pay for treatment, and they fall into debt in a single bad month. With no pension, old age arrives as poverty.

What follows

The plan shares the cost. Employers contribute their part, the state co-sponsors contributions in a new firm’s first two years, and workers pay a small share. The aim is complete health-insurance and provident-fund enrolment inside every designated park.

THE SYSTEMIC TRAP

These five bottlenecks reinforce each other into a vicious cycle. Land delays block capital; contract precarity forces out-migration; transit deficits exclude women. No piecemeal subsidy works—all five must be broken simultaneously.

THE ECONOMIC THRESHOLD

Local industrial policy cannot succeed with marginal training or sub-minimum wages. A viable intervention must offer formal 3-year contracts paying >= ₹14,000 to beat out-of-state remittances after accounting for metro living costs.

THE SYNCHRONIZED RESPONSE

Project Udyog Bihar is engineered not as a generic subsidy, but as an integrated institutional vehicle synchronizing turnkey infrastructure, legal contracts, women transit, and automated telemetry to spark domestic dignity.

Why all five at onceIn plain words

A bottleneck is the narrowest point in a process, the point that decides how much can flow through everything else. Remove a different obstacle and nothing speeds up until the narrow point is opened. That is why the paper lists five and insists on dealing with them together.

The evidence from elsewhere

Other places that fixed only one link show what happens. Vietnam built industrial parks along highways but did not enforce contracts, and most young factory workers were hired without them, so informal work simply moved into a modern building. Ethiopia built a showcase park, but wages were too low to cover food and housing and there was no childcare, so workers quit within about three months. Indian ITIs trained people without any hiring link and produced certified, jobless graduates.

How Bihar’s five link up

Land delays keep factories from opening, so firms stay small and informal. Informality means no contracts, so workers see no reason to stay, and they migrate. Missing transit and crèches keep women out, so half the potential workforce never applies. Unreliable power raises costs, and slow, discretionary subsidies invite fraud. Each failure feeds the others, which is why the response has to be one coordinated plan.

How to read the benchmarksIn plain words

Each card sets out a mechanism, a result and a lesson. We did not copy any of these places. We asked what each one actually did, what it achieved, what it got wrong and which part transfers to Bihar.

Bangladesh

Garment factories sited close to where people live brought millions of women into paid work. The lesson for Bihar is to place factories near homes. What does not apply is the coastline: Bangladesh ships goods by sea, while Bihar is landlocked and should aim at India’s large home market.

Vietnam

Highway-side parks made moving goods cheap, but contracts were not enforced. Bihar should copy the location logic and not the labour practices.

Ethiopia

A large park failed to keep its workers because pay did not cover living costs and there was no childcare. Bihar’s version of that lesson is the wage floor: a local job has to beat what a migrant nets elsewhere.

Tamil Nadu and Amul

Tamil Nadu’s industrial agency acted as master developer, buying land and building utilities and ready sheds so that firms could simply move in. Amul’s dairy cooperatives sent most of the retail price of milk back to farmers. Together they point to a state that builds the base and to farmers who share in the value of what they grow, which matters for Bihar’s maize, makhana and litchi.

The plan in plain languageIn plain words
A different role for the state

For decades industrial policy meant leasing bare plots and hoping an investor would handle permits, power and construction. That takes years. Under Project Udyog Bihar the state acts as an active accelerator. It builds the factory first, then invites firms in.

Four moves

Build first. Ready, pre-wired “plug-and-play” sheds at a low rent per square foot cut the time to start production from years to months.

Go where the pain is. Early money goes to the districts with the worst graduate unemployment.

Enforce real jobs. Support depends on written three-year contracts at a tiered living wage.

Open the door to women. Every park has shuttles and a crèche.

Why it can be trusted

Subsidies reach workers only after independent records confirm that the company filed its tax returns and deposited provident-fund dues. If a firm dismisses a contracted worker early without approval, it must refund the support. Ghost firms and fake hiring are the usual way such schemes fail, and the checks are designed to make them impossible to run profitably.

How the money is split

Roughly half of public capital goes to infrastructure: sheds, power and shared waste treatment. About a third supports wages and benefits for contracted workers. A smaller share pays for women’s transport and childcare, and the rest funds testing laboratories and the digital monitoring that keeps the whole system honest.

Why these wagesIn plain words
The idea of a floor

Each worker compares his local offer with what he can bring home by leaving. If the local job pays less than that, he leaves again. The wage floor is the lowest pay at which staying makes sense.

Where the numbers come from

A typical Bihar household spends around ₹12,400 a month on living costs and brings in around ₹14,900. A new factory worker who is paid less than about ₹15,000 cannot meet the family’s ordinary expenses and still save. The three tiers, from assembly operator through technician to graduate and supervisor, step upward so that skills are rewarded and a graduate feels the degree was worth it.

Why sector choice matters

Not every factory raises living standards. In Bihar today, small manufacturing units are among the lowest-paying employers, below construction and far below services such as IT and health care. A graduate will not accept pay under what her family needs just to eat. The sectors the plan recruits, including food processing, technical apparel, electronics and digital services, are those whose margins let them pay the wage floor.

Reading the rolloutIn plain words
Phase 1: build

In the first year, resources go to six crisis clusters. At each, the state delivers a turnkey park on land it already holds, with industrial electricity, internet, solar power, shared waste treatment, a crèche and safe transit stops. Using existing industrial land and closed sugar-mill grounds avoids the years of dispute that private land purchase can bring.

Phase 2: recruit and enforce

With sheds ready, the state recruits firms, including garment makers, food processors and electronics assemblers from other states. It co-funds a share of wages for new local graduates for two years, provided the employer signs a three-year contract at or above the wage floor. It also reimburses half of the employer’s provident-fund and insurance contributions on those contracts.

Phase 3: deepen

Later years build the ecosystem around the parks: neighbourhood stitching and packaging workshops in towns, run on the model of the JEEViKA women’s self-help groups, shared testing laboratories for food and materials, and ITIs reorganised so students spend half their course on real factory floors.

Land disputes, answered by building only on pre-cleared industrial land. Subsidy cheating, answered by payment only after tax and provident-fund records confirm real activity. Workers quitting and migrating again, answered by the wage floor. Training that leads nowhere, answered by releasing funds only for verified placements. Women unable to take jobs, answered by buses and crèches in every park.

Where this leaves BiharIn plain words

The conclusion of the study is that Bihar’s problem is not a lack of talent, work ethic or ambition. It is a structural shortage of formal, well-organised jobs. One in four young graduates is looking for work and cannot find it. Nearly two in three salaried workers have no contract. Most educated women are shut out. Families survive on the earnings of fathers and sons in distant factories. Building the factories at home, with contracts, a living wage and access for women, gives that education somewhere to land.

How the data was preparedData & definitions

The figures come from the Periodic Labour Force Survey of the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. Government surveyors visit households across the country and record two sets of information: one on each person (age, schooling, work, contract, earnings) and one on the household as a whole (size, spending, remittances received). The raw files cannot simply be added up, so eight preparation rules were applied before any conclusion was drawn.

The eight rules

1. Isolate Bihar. National averages wash out local problems, so only the 85,505 interviews from Bihar’s districts were used.

2. Match each person to the right household. Sixteen geographic and visit codes were used together so that families are not mixed up or counted twice.

3. Apply population weights. Each interview represents a number of citizens, and multiplying by that weight scales the sample up to the state’s roughly 110 million people.

4. Use the standard definition of unemployment. Only people working or actively seeking work are in the labour force. Students, retirees and full-time carers are not counted as jobless.

5. Separate the young. People aged 15–29 were analysed by level of schooling, because pooling all ages hides where the problem is worst.

6. Look beyond the word “salaried.” Workers were checked for a written contract, its length, and provident-fund or health cover.

7. Standardise wages. Daily pay was converted to a 30-day month so that casual and salaried work can be compared, and monthly earnings were capped at ₹1.5 lakh so that a few very high earners do not distort averages.

8. Link work choices to remittances. Household records of money received from relatives elsewhere were joined to individual records, to show how outside money shapes what local jobs people accept.

Key terms

PLFS: the national household survey of employment. Labour force: people working or looking for work. Unemployment rate: job-seekers as a share of the labour force. LFPR: labour force participation rate, the share of working-age people who work or look for work. Reservation wage: the lowest pay a person will accept. Remittance: money sent home by a migrant relative. BIADA: Bihar Industrial Area Development Authority, which builds and manages industrial estates. ITI: Industrial Training Institute. EPFO / PF: the provident-fund body and the retirement account it manages. ESI: state health insurance for workers. DBT: direct benefit transfer, a payment sent straight to a bank account. PFMS: the government’s public financial management system. JEEViKA: Bihar’s women’s self-help group network. Plug-and-play shed: a factory building delivered with power, water and road access already installed.
